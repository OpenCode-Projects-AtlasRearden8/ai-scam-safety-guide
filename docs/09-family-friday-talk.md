# 09: The Family Friday Talk

You've read the chapters. Now the people you love need the same ten
percent of it that protects them. This is a literal, gentle outline for a
half-hour family conversation — around the kitchen table, on a Saturday
morning, or as Friday pizza is cooling. It works for talking to parents,
teens, spouses, and grandparents, and it has no scary-movie energy.

## The ground rules (read these before the talk)

- **Not a lecture.** A family meeting. Bring snacks; end on time.
- **No blame, no quizzes.** Nobody is being tested, nobody's past losses
  are brought up unless they want to share.
- **Small agenda, big item.** One new habit gets set up tonight: the
  family passphrase. Everything else is shared context.

## The 30-minute outline

**1. Open (3 minutes).** "I read a guide about the new scam calls and
photos — the ones that use AI to sound like a real person. Ten minutes
now saves everybody trouble later. And there's one thing we need to
agree on together."

**2. One story, told gently (5 minutes).** Read one story from
[the story examples](stories.md) out loud — the grandkid-voice one
usually lands. Nobody in the room is the villain; the scammer is a
calling-farm company.

**3. The three moves (7 minutes).** Say these plainly and post them on
the fridge:

- **We hang up and call back.** Any call or text about money, trouble,
  codes, or "verify who you are" — we hang up and call the person on the
  number we already have. Even if the voice is exactly them.
- **We never read codes to anyone.** Verification codes stay with us. No
  "read me the code" ever has a good reason.
- **We ask about money out loud.** Nobody in this family wires,
  gift-cards, or cryptos a stranger, in private. If a real deal is real
  tonight, it's real tomorrow.

**4. Set the family passphrase (7 minutes).** Explain it as something
sweet, not frightened: "how we know it's really each other." Pick it
together — two random words, not names, dates, pets, or anything on a
public feed. Write it on two index cards; each household keeps one in a
drawer (not a photo on a phone). Then set the rule: any emergency call
that involves money or secrecy means asking for the passphrase — no
hard feelings if it's not remembered, because asking is love, not
suspicion.

**5. Two-minute role play (5 minutes).** Warn the players, then have one
person "call" another: "Grandma? It's me — I broke my phone, I'm using a
borrowed one, I need bail money, please don't tell Dad." Practice the
response out loud: "Oh honey — what's the family passphrase? ... Okay,
I'm calling you right back on your usual number." Laugh, reset. Doing it
once in a warm room makes the real moment muscle memory.

**6. Close warm (3 minutes).** "This isn't because anyone's dumb. These
scams are made in factories. We're doing this because we're a team."
Then, snacks, or whatever comes next on a Friday.

## Versions for tricky rooms

- **Grandparents who "won't fall for it."** Lead with respect and
  facts: "These calls now use a clone of your own voice to a grandson;
  the guide shows a transcript." Hands-on beats instruction — offer to
  save your number in their phone as "Do answer: this is really Dylan,"
  and put the call-back rule on one index card by their phone.
- **Teens (they're the target too, especially by sextortion).** Lead
  with their side of it: never read codes to anyone, never send photos
  to *anyone* that you'd rather not see pasted at school, and that
  shame-free call to you at any hour is always the winning move. Promise
  the no-punishment part and keep it.
- **A parent with various stages of forgetfulness.** Simplify to a
  single posted sentence: **"Hang up. Call me back at [ your number
  ]. Never pay by gift card."** Repetition in a warm voice, monthly,
  works better than one thorough explanation.
- **A skeptical spouse.** Frame as boring insurance: "twenty minutes and
  a code word, and then we never talk about it this thoroughly again."

## The follow-up cadence

Once a month, over any meal: one sentence — "Anyone gotten a weird
call or message this month?" Two minutes per answer, no judgments,
plan nothing else. The ritual matters more than the content: it makes
"asking the family about a strange message" normal, which is exactly
the reflex the factories are engineered to defeat.

Print [the checklist](checklist.md) and let whoever hangs it choose the
wall. The night is done when the passphrase exists and someone said
"okay, that actually makes me feel better."
