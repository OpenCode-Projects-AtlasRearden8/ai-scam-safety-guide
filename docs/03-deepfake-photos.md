# 03: Deepfake Photos

A deepfake photo is an image of something that never happened: a face on a
profile, a product on a listing, a screenshot of a "cash app payment," a
smiling figure holding a check with your name on it. AI image generators
are so good that the old advice — "look at the hands!" — is now only *one*
check among several.

This chapter covers the three places fake photos actually do damage:
dating/friendship profiles, marketplace and product listings, and "proof of
payment" screenshots.

## The visual tells (use more than one, in this order)

1. **Hands and objects.** Hands have improved but still struggle: six
   fingers, a phone melting into a palm, jewelry that passes through
   fingers, coffee cups held from inside the liquid.
2. **Text inside the image.** Logos, name tags, receipts, signs behind the
   subject. AI is weakest at small crisp text. If an ID badge or receipt is
   the "proof," zoom in and check every letter.
3. **Light direction mismatch.** The face is lit from the left, the shadows
   fall right, or two sunlight sources hit the same coffee cup from
   opposite sides.
4. **Too-even skin and hair.** Flawless skin on a 70-year-old, hair with an
   oddly clean hairline against a busy background, teeth too symmetric.
5. **Background logic.** A window whose view doesn't match the room,
   reflections that show a different scene, chairs with melting legs.

**Important:** one clean-looking photo proves nothing either way. The
point of these checks is to find something *wrong*. If you find one true
glitch, trust it more than ten things that look fine.

## The context tells (these catch what eyes miss)

- **Reverse image search.** On a phone or computer, use a reverse image
  search (Google Lens or a similar tool) on the person's or the product's
  photo. A profile photo that turns up on a stock-photography site, a
  modeling portfolio, or fifteen different LinkedIn profiles is a stock
  image or a stolen identity — either way, not the person you're talking to.
- **Profile age and math.** Accounts created eight days ago with three
  posts of polished photos and instant intimacy ("I really connect with
  you") are doing a job. Real people of a certain age have a messy
  history: old posts, argument replies, company photos nobody would stage.
- **The conversation asks.** Fake profiles exist to move you somewhere
  private, warm you up over weeks, and then produce a crisis. The photos
  are the bait; the timing of the ask is the tell (see [the "sweet
  stranger" story](stories.md)).

## Checking a product photo or listing

- **The listing photo set.** Real sellers' photos share one camera: same
  room, same lighting, wear consistent across shots. AI-generated or stolen
  photo sets often mix lighting, backgrounds, or focus styles.
- **The too-clean price.** A $900 espresso machine, new, for $119, with
  photos of what looks like a showroom: chapter 06 covers why "yes, it's a
  deal" is often the whole scam.
- **Copy-paste descriptions.** Text that reads generic ("Great addition to
  your home, this amazing product will bring the family joy") with zero
  mention of the model number or what's in the box is template text — often
  machine-written and endlessly reused. Search a distinctive sentence in
  quotes.

## Screenshots pretending to be proof

"Proof of payment" screenshots are trivially fakeable even without AI:

- No bank shows deposits the way the screenshot shows them, standard
  details like the date format are off, or the app's font and color are "close."
- If someone claims they paid you or your business, **wait for the money to
  land in your actual account** before doing your part of any deal. The
  balance on *your* bank's app is the only proof that matters.

## A calm rule of thumb

> **Photos are claims, not facts.** Nothing on the internet becomes safer
> because a picture of it looks good. Verify through a channel you control:
> search the image, check the account's history, and never let a photo do a
> person's job of proving identity.

If it's a *voice* you're being asked to trust — a call, not a photo — that's
the next chapter, and it's the one most worth reading in this entire guide.
