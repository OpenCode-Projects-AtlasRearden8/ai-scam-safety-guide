# Story Examples

Six stories, told the way the attacks actually arrive, with the tells
marked. Names and details are invented composites of widely reported
patterns — no real persons or incidents. Read one at a family meeting or
in three spare minutes; each is deliberately short.

## 1. The Voice on the Phone (cloned voice + family passphrase)

Margaret's phone rings at 7:40 p.m. Her grandson's voice, young and
frantic: "Grandma, it's Tyler — I'm in a county jail outside Columbus.
There was a wreck; I hit someone. I need $4,200 for the bond today, the
bondsman keeps the cash. Please don't tell Dad, he'll hear about the
driving."

A man on the line identifies himself: "Attorney Reiss, I'm handling
Tyler's case — the court has a strict 5 p.m. window for bond, but our
system takes a wire by 3."

The voice is Tyler's. Margaret had always trusted her ears, and every
instinct in her room said *go*. She asks one question:

"Tyler, honey — what's the family passphrase?"

Pause. A shuffling sound. The "attorney" jumps in: "Ma'am, the police
need a yes right now or we lose the cell —"

She hangs up. Calls Tyler on the number she's had since he was born. He
answers from his desk, mid-bowling-lane nachos.

**The tells:** a crisis-only-cash, a channel-protected "don't tell," a
stranger with a stopwatch, and an objection to any verification.
**The move:** the passphrase, then the call-back.
**The line for the fridge:** a real emergency is a real emergency ten
minutes later.

## 2. The Perfect Seller (too-good deal + screenshot proof)

Jon wanted a particular table saw; used ones ran $600–700. A marketplace
listing had one at $220, "moving in two weeks."

Photos looked professional — but the grain of the wood shifted color
between shots. The seller's account had been made that week. Five
reviews of the seller praised an engagement ring, never a saw.

Then the pitch: "To hold it for you, Zelle $50 now — I've already
accepted the funds layer, look." A screenshot. Amount: $0.00 pending. A
nickname in the memo line. Jon *almost* sent the $50.

**The tells:** price math that had no explanation, a one-week-old
account, photo-set lighting mismatches, reviews from another product's
life, and a screenshot pretending to be a payment.
**The move:** "Great — I'll pay when it's in my hands," and reporting
the listing with the marketplace's own button.

## 3. Notely, the Little AI App (fake AI tool + password reuse)

Priya's feed showed an ad: "Notely — your papers, summarized by AI.
Free!" The screenshots looked like a real product.

She signed up with her main email and — being one human with habits — a
password she had used in three other places. The app asked for "email
access (optional, for feature-full experience)." She left. Then she
forgot about the site.

Three weeks later, a "new device sign-in" arrived for Priya's other
account: the fake tool kept the password, and machines tried it against
every site where she'd reused it.

**The tells:** an ad-sourced domain, a signup that allowed nonsense, a
permission ask no note-taker needs.
**The move:** the throwaway-account rule (chapter 05). Priya spent that
evening changing passwords and turning on two-factor everywhere —
*The Privacy Comfort Handbook* covers that neat little ladder in its
chapter 03.

## 4. The 4:55 Text From the Boss (business email compromise)

Dana's phone buzzed at 4:55, fifteen minutes before close: "Hi Dana, in
my budget meeting. Please process the attached invoice before the
vendor's cutoff — their banking changed today. Confidential, my phone is
out of plan minutes; text only."

Attached: a PDF styled to look like their usual biller, with a new
account number at 100% of the form's width, in a font a shade thinner.

Dana had read this guide on a Saturday in a waiting room. One rule
punchline lived in her head: **a change of payment details is verified
by calling a number you already had.** She called the vendor's office
number from the contract. The vendor's own bank account hadn't changed
in eleven years.

**The tells:** off-hours urgency, channel narrowing ("text only"),
perfectly styled paperwork with one new detail, and a request that dies
instantly when the known number is called.
**The move:** the invoice-change rule, rung three of the verification
ladder (chapter 07).

## 5. The Sweet Stranger (fake profile, slow-warm scam)

A friendly person from a photo-perfect profile sent Mara a warm note
about a book she'd mentioned in public. The profile was six weeks old;
the photos showed one very good-looking person in five very different
lifestyles (mountains, private jet interior, a consistent-but-unreal
golden-hour glow).

Weeks of lovely conversation followed — always just slightly vague on
real details, always quick to move off the app into a
private chat. Then, in month two, a "can't-lose" investment platform
"Mara could join too, we've done so well."

Mara's check list: reverse-search of two photos showed them as stock
images. The account was three posts deep. The platform they blessed had
a registration age of nine days.

**The tells:** stock-photo face, new account, instant intimacy, and a
money platform that only exists inside their story.
**The move:** none of the conversation was wasted — Mara simply stopped
replying and reported the profile. No money, no drama, one small evening.

## 6. The Grant That Needed $97 First (pay-to-receive)

An email with a university-looking header: "Your application
mysteriously qualifies for the Freedman Community Grant, $4,000 — a
small processing fee of $97 releases the check, fully refundable."

The masthead was clean; the fee was the scam. Grants process fees after
money arrives, and they don't come from a mailshot for people who never
applied. The reply address had an inbox full of other people saying
"okay."

**The tells:** pay-to-receive, fee-before-money order, and eligibility
without application.
**The move:** delete and don't look back — and the calm phrase from
chapter 07: money only flows one way in honest deals.
