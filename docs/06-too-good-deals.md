# 06: Too-Good Deals

The too-good deal has always been around. What's new is that AI lets a
fake shop look *perfect*: flawless product photos in three angles,
soothing copy, hundreds of five-star reviews, a professional-looking
checkout — all generated in bulk for pennies. The polish is no longer a
signal of trust. The tells that remain live in the numbers and the
payment method.

## The basic math

One question does most of the work in this chapter:

> **How is this price possible?**

A brand-new electric bike that retails for $1,900, listed at $240 by a
store you've never heard of, has two possible explanations: a miracle, or
a hook. Miracles are rare; hooks are a business. If the discount doesn't
survive that one question, walk away — there's no shame in leaving money
on the table of a deal you can't explain.

## The listing tells

- **The store is new and vague.** "About us" with generic synonym salad,
  no address, no phone, contact only through a web form or a Gmail
  address.
- **The domain is odd.** Hyphen-stacked, brand-adjacent
  (nike-clearance-us.net), or registered recently. You rarely need to look
  up registration dates; "does the store exist anywhere but this site?" is
  the faster question.
- **Everything is discounted forever.** Countdown timers that reset,
  "sale ends in 3 hours" that has been true for three weeks (check using
  a private/incognito window — timers frequently reset for fresh visitors).
- **Reviews that aren't attached to real purchases.** Same cadence of
  praise across different names ("Excellent quality! Fast shipping!
  Will buy again!"), no mid-range (3-star) reviews at all, or reviews
  that describe a *different product* than the one listed — a classic sign
  of junk generated from templates.
- **Payment terms doing the heavy lifting.** "Wire transfer or Zelle only
  for this deal, because of fees," or "Venmo to this *personal* account,
  the business account is down." See the payment traffic light in
  [chapter 07](07-verifying-people-and-payments.md).

## Platforms with buyer protection vs. raw transfers

| Venue | Protection if the item never shows |
| --- | --- |
| Card purchase (not debit) on a real marketplace site | Chargeback possible; banks take fraud seriously |
| PayPal "purchase" flows | Buyer protection, with rules and deadlines |
| Zelle, Venmo, Cash App *to a stranger*, gift cards, wire, crypto | Essentially none; treat as cash handed to a stranger |

Rule: **buy with payment methods that can be reversed, from venues where
disputes are a five-minute form.** When a seller steers you away from
those toward irreversible rails, they are telling you something, loudly.

## The used-marketplace special

Marketplace app scams deserve three specifics:

- **The verification-code trick.** "To prove *you're* real, read me the
  code I just sent you." That code logs *them* into your phone number or
  account. Never read verification codes to anyone—ever—for any reason.
- **The deposit-and-disappear.** A too-cheap item "can be held for you"
  with a small deposit. Deposits are the modern handshake only when both
  people can be verified; with a stranger, it's a paid goodbye.
- **The fake-shipping route.** They want to "handle shipping" through a
  site/app you've never heard of, which will happily take your money and
  disappear with it.

## When it's a service, not a thing

"Book this cabin for $80/night (comparable ones: $350)." Fake-booking
sites use photos of real cabins that aren't theirs. If the price gap is
that size, search the identical photo (see chapter 03) and check whether
the same cabin is listed by a venue you recognize at real prices.

## The calm exit

A good deal will survive one careful evening. Sleep on it, search the
store name plus "scam," and recompute the math tomorrow. If it evaporates
overnight, it was a hook, and the only thing you lost was the feeling of
almost getting rich quick — which is exactly the feeling the scam was
selling, and cheapest to lose now.
