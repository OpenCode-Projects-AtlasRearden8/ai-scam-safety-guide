# 02: Spotting AI-Written Scams

An ordinary scam relies on you mistaking fake text for a real person. That
was easier in 2015. The fix today is not squinting at the grammar — it's
testing the *context*. Machines write fluent text; they don't yet live our
lives.

## The five tells that survive perfect grammar

**1. The name/context mismatch.** The message says "Hi Dylan!" but comes
from an account signed "We fixed your window washers?" or references a
detail that's almost right: a school you didn't attend, a boss with a name
one letter off. AI writes well about a template; it errs on the details
*around* the words.

**2. Urgency with a deadline.** "Respond within 24 hours or the account is
closed." Real institutions do not run your life on countdown timers. The
faster the clock, the harder you should slow down.

**3. The switch.** "Sorry, I'm about to lose signal — can you text me at
..." or "let's move to this app instead." The switch exists to take the
conversation somewhere the rules (and the scam-spotting moment) are weaker.

**4. The impossible ask, said politely.** "Could you send it via Apple
gift card?" "Wire it to this new account today." No legitimate person or
business needs a scam-shaped payment method (see the traffic-light table in
[chapter 07](07-verifying-people-and-payments.md)).

**5. The appearance of a relationship you never had.** "With respect to your
order I refunded the wrong amount" — flimsy pronouns, missing specifics.
The message feels fluent but not *felt*. A real person's message about your
issue usually contains at least one detail you actually know is true.

## The two tests — a minute, no tools

**The reply test.** Reply with a simple, off-script question: "Which part of
the order — where did I order it?" or "I don't remember what the invoice
was for." A real person answers with something true. A script (human or
bot) answers vaguely, smoothly, or by repeating the same ask. One silly,
specific, unanswerable question does more detection work than any tool.

**The name test.** Search the sender's name exactly as it appears, plus
"scam" or "letters." Or paste the suspicious text into a search engine in
quotes; most scam messages are derived from a small number of templates,
and the search box is the fastest copy-machine detector most of us have.

## Two worked examples

**The believable kind (school district, AI-polished):**

> "Hello Dylan, We hope this message finds you well. Please review the
> revised schedule attached. Due to a system compile, some routes have been
> reassigned for tomorrow. Kindly confirm receipt below."

Meaningless "system compile," vague "revised schedule," and generic
"confirm receipt below." A real district email would name the school, the
route number, and the bus plan you recognize. The tells: vague nouns +
one invented term, and urgency about nothing in particular.

**The emotional kind (grandkid text):**

> "Hey it's me!! My phone broke I'm using a friend's — lost my wallet, can
> you get the new one tonight? I'm with a new person, I'm shaking. I'll
> explain later, didn't want to call bc they have my photos."

Copied intimacy, hardware-failure excuse for why you can't verify, and
payment tonight. Those three together are the classic modern family
emergency — explained properly in [chapter 04](04-cloned-voice-calls.md).

## What to do when a message trips your alarm

- **Don't click anything**, don't open attachments, don't call the number
  printed *inside* the suspicious message (it's part of the trap).
- If it claims to be your bank, utility, or delivery service, contact them
  through the phone number on your statement, your card, or the app you
  already use — this one habit is worth more than anything else in this
  chapter.
- If it came to one of your accounts as a message (email, Facebook, a
  marketplace listing), report it with the platform's own "report" link.
  Even if you're 90% sure it's real, reporting a false positive costs them
  nothing; ignoring a real scam costs someone.
- Mark it done. Being alert is the goal, not spending your evening worried.

## Where the deeper link anatomy lives

The clicks, the slightly-wrong domains, the "you have a package" hooks —
the mechanical guts of link-based phishing — are covered chapter by chapter
in *The Privacy Comfort Handbook* (chapter 04). Come back here from there:

- **The rest of *this* handbook** is about the *people-looking* side: faces,
  voices, and deals. Machines write fluently; humans invent meaning. Judge
  the meaning.

One-line summary for the fridge: **if it's fluent, urgent, and slightly
off-script in the details, it was probably written by a machine that has
never met either of you.**
