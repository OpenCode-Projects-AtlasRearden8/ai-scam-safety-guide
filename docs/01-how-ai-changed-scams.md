# 01: How AI Changed Scams

For decades, scam emails arrived with the warning label built in: broken
grammar, odd spellings, a prince with money to move. We got comfortable
skimming them because they *looked* wrong.

Generative AI took the built-in warning label off.

## What actually got better: three things

**1. The writing.** A scam message used to read like a translation; now it
reads like your coworker, your kid's school, or your credit union. Large
language models produce fluent, polite, correctly capitalized text on demand,
and a scammer can generate a thousand variations before lunch.

**2. The pictures.** Product photos, headshots, ID badges, screenshots —
machines can generate convincing versions of all of them in seconds. The
old advice "look for weird hands" is helpful but no longer complete
(chapter 03).

**3. The voice.** A few seconds of audio — a voicemail greeting, a clip from
a public video — is enough to build a voice model that can speak as you, on
the phone (chapter 04).

## What did *not* change: the anatomy

Here's the reassuring part. Strip away the polish and every scam is the same
three-part machine:

1. **A story** that makes you feel something fast — fear, greed, love,
   duty, or urgency.
2. **A channel move** — "switch to text, or to WhatsApp, or keep this
   private," so the request leaves the place where normal rules apply.
3. **A payment or data ask** — usually one the regular world would choke on,
   like gift cards, crypto, wire, or "share the code I just texted you."

If you can spot any one of those three parts, you've caught the scam, no
matter how good the grammar is.

## The two-second rules that still work

These come up in every chapter that follows, so put them on your shelf now:

> **The pause rule.** Nothing real requires a legal decision, a payment, or a
> passphrase inside of two minutes. Urgency is the scammer's product, not
> their problem.

> **The channel rule.** When someone contacts you out of the blue and asks
> for something sensitive, you end the conversation and contact them back
> using a phone number or website *you* already had. You are not calling
> back a scammer; you are starting a fresh, verifiable conversation.

> **The payment rule.** Gift cards, crypto, wire transfers, and any
> "refundable fee" are scam payments. Stores know this; that's why those
> cashiers who ask "is anyone telling you to buy this?" exist.

## Why "it won't happen to me" is dangerous — and why it isn't shameful

Being targeted isn't a judgment on your intelligence. These attacks are
professional products aimed at everyone: the newest scam kit can be rented
for the price of a pizza. Smart, careful people fall for AI-graded scams
every day — then quietly feel ashamed, which is exactly what keeps the
industry going.

If that ever happens to you, chapter 08 is waiting, and it starts from the
assumption that you're okay.
