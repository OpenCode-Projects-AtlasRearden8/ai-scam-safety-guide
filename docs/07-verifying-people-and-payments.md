# 07: Verifying People and Payments

So far this guide has been about spotting fakes. This chapter is the
positive skill: proving, quickly and kindly, that the person asking you
for something is real — and paying in ways that keep you safe even if
you're wrong.

## The verification ladder

Use as many rungs as the stakes require. A dinner invitation needs rung
one; a wire transfer deserves rung three.

**Rung 1 — Restate on the channel you chose.** They emailed? You call the
number in your contact list. They texted from a "new number"? You text the
*old* number and ask, "Are you on a new phone?" (This one question is the
single most effective anti-voice-clone move there is.)

**Rung 2 — Ask for shared knowledge.** Not "who are you?" but something
only the two of you would know: "What did we argue about on the boat
trip?" A real friend answers with a specific, imperfect memory. A
scammer — or a machine trained on a real person's public life — answers
generically. If you don't share history, rung 1 or the family passphrase
(chapter 04) applies instead.

**Rung 3 — Third-party confirm.** For money matters, confirm through a
source that has no relationship to the requester: your bank's fraud line,
the organization's number from their official statement or website, a
mutual person. Never a number or link that arrived *in the request* —
that's part of the scam furniture.

## The invoice-change rule (for business and family bookkeeping alike)

> **Any change to payment details — new account number, new "payment
> email," different country for the wire — must be verified by calling a
> phone number you already had.**

This one rule kills the scam called business email compromise, where an
email that reads exactly like your landlord's, boss's, or accountant's
says "our old bank account has issues, wire this month's payment to the
new one below." The writing will be flawless. The rule doesn't care.

## The payment traffic light

| Color | Methods | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 🟢 Green | Card (not debit) purchase, PayPal goods-and-services, the platform's own checkout | Buyer protection exists; use it |
| 🟡 Yellow | Zelle/Venmo/Cash App to someone you know *and* have verified some other way; in-person cash with a receipt | Fine in daylight with a real identity |
| 🔴 Red | Gift cards, wire to a stranger, crypto, paying to *receive* money, "refundable" deposits to unknown accounts | Nobody sincere ever needs these |

Two extra observations that close most gaps:

- **Money only flows one way in honest deals.** "Send $50 to release your
  $2,000 grant" is not how money works anywhere.
- **Overpayment is a scam shape, not a clerical error.** A stranger
  "accidentally" pays you too much and asks you to refund the difference
  — the original payment then bounces or was fake, and your refund is
  real money you gave away.

## When you're the one selling or receiving

- If you sell online, platforms' built-in payment holds beat any zealous
  "I already sent the funds" screenshot. Money lands; then you ship (and
  screenshots of payment are cover-fodder — see chapter 03).
- For face-to-face cash deals, daytime, daylight, a public place, and a
  friend in the car are worth more than any in-app messaging.
- If someone insists their "money is already showing as pending," the
  polite and safe reply is: **"Then it'll be great when it arrives."**
  Pending never arrives for a stranger who wants you to ship early.

## A minute on "they're offended that I'm verifying"

Real people respect verification. Your mechanic, your bank, your
grandkid, your boss — everyone who matters gets it when you say "give me
two minutes to make sure." The people who get *angry* about being
verified, or who argue you into skipping it, are doing the work of the
scam themselves.
