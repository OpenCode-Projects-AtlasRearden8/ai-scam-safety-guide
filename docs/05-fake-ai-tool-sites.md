# 05: Fake AI Tool Sites

AI made everyone want the tools — and scammers opened fake toolshops to catch
that demand. This chapter is about sites and apps dressed as ChatGPT,
image generators, "free AI resume writers," and browser add-ons, whose real
product is **your data**: logins, card numbers, or the contents of
whatever you paste.

The reason this works is simple: people are eager and trusting with
anything labeled "AI," especially when it's free.

## The five shapes this scam takes

**1. The typo'd domain.** `chatgpt-demo-support.com`, `openai-chat.chat`,
`gptfree4you.io` — a coworker's link or a search ad that looks official
until you read the middle of the URL. Nearly all of the real ones live at
domains their company explains on their own site; if you can't confirm the
domain from official material you already trust, the site is
untrustworthy by definition.

**2. The free trial that isn't.** You use the fake tool once for free; the
paywall arrives only after you've typed your card number "to verify." The
service produces something generic; your card keeps a $1 "verification"
charge, then more.

**3. The login farm.** The fake site shows a "Sign in with Google/Microsoft"
button — and it can be real, or a copy of one. The protection is the same:
any sign-in that happens on a site you did not reach from the official
app or site can be credential theater. When
you sign in anywhere, look at the **address bar**, not the buttons: is the
login page actually the provider's own domain?

**4. The harvest app.** Add-ons look innocent; they ask for wide permissions
("read and change all data on all sites"), or a phone app asks for
contacts, mic, files it doesn't need. Fake AI note-takers and keyboard
apps love "access to all texts."

**5. The upgrade trap inside the real chatbot.** "You've reached your
free limit — upgrade for $1." Fake pages copy the look of real products
astonishingly well. If it arrived as a link (email, text, ad), the upgrade
is fake; upgrades belong *inside* the account you signed into from the
official site, not inside a page that opened after your tab was refreshed.

## What "harvested data" actually costs

The privacy handbook covers the deep rules of what never to paste anywhere
— but here is the scammer's side of the trade:

- You paste a document → they own whatever's in it (names, numbers,
  contracts).
- You sign in with your main email password → they now try that password
  everywhere else you've reused it. This is why the *privacy handbook* is
  so stubborn about one good password per site plus 2FA: it makes a fake
  tool site nearly worthless to thieves.
- You give card details → the $1 turns into mystery charges, and the card
  is on a list.

## The six checks before you use any AI tool

1. **Where did you hear about it?** A search-result *ad* or a friend's
   forwarded link is the *weakest* source. An official site, an app store
   listing by the well-known publisher, or a trusted newsletter is
   stronger.
2. **Read the domain out loud.** Slowly, letter by letter, for the part
   between `https://` and the first `/`. Typosquats bank on you skimming.
3. **Does the publisher exist elsewhere?** Their real site, their GitHub
   org, their track record. A tool with no history and no working contact
   page has no real footprint.
4. **Try the empty test.** Give the site a fake name, fake email, fake
   password before you sign up for real. If it accepts nonsense happily,
   it's a form, not a service.
5. **Check the asks.** Permissions that don't match the job: a font
   generator that wants your messages, a background remover that wants
   your contacts — leave.
6. **Use a sandbox account.** For any new tool, make a free email address
   just for signups, and never enter a password you've used anywhere else.
   If the tool is real, it takes two minutes longer; if it's fake, it
   takes two minutes of your time and nothing else.

## Sticky-note version

> Before you trust an AI tool site: **who told you the address, what does
> the domain say when you read it out loud, and what is being asked?**
> New tools get a throwaway account and a throwaway password — never your
> main one.
