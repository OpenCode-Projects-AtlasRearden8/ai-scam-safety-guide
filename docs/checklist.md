# The Scam-Spotting Checklist

Print this page and put it where family eyes land — fridge, noticeboard,
by the phone. It's the whole guide compressed into one sheet.

## Before responding to any surprising message or call

- [ ] **Pause.** Nothing real needs a decision, payment, or code inside two minutes.
- [ ] **Check the details around the words:** does the name, school, boss, order, or route match your real life exactly? An "almost right" detail is why the message was cheap to make.
- [ ] **Notice the urgency.** Deadlines, threats of closure, "final notices today" — urgency is the product, not information.
- [ ] **Do the reply test.** Ask one specific, off-script question ("which part of the order? Where'd I order it?"). Vague or smooth answers are the machine telling on itself.
- [ ] **End on your terms and re-verify:** hang up / close, then contact them back on a number or site you already had. Never a number or link from the message itself.

## If the message asks for money or codes (or secrecy)

- [ ] Gift cards, crypto, wire to a stranger, Zelle/Venmo to someone unknown, paying to *receive* money, "refundable deposits" — none of these can be part of a good transaction.
- [ ] Never read a verification code to anyone, for any reason — it is the key to your account.
- [ ] Any change to payment details ("new bank account," different email for invoices) gets verified by phone call to the number you already have. Every time.
- [ ] Asked for the family passphrase? No passphrase, no move — hang up and call back.
- [ ] Someone "already sent you money" and wants a refund or early shipping? It hasn't arrived until it's in your balance, viewed in *your own* banking app.

## Before trusting a voice

- [ ] **Love the voice, hang up anyway.** A few seconds of audio is enough to clone it; the ear is no longer a security check.
- [ ] Borrowed phone / dead battery / "don't tell Mom" / a stranger "officer" or "lawyer" joins — classic cloned-voice crisis furniture.
- [ ] Call the person on your own contact list (or their workplace, sibling, school). Two real people beat one fake voice.

## Before trusting a photo, profile, or listing

- [ ] Reverse-search the photo — faces on a stock site or many profiles means it's not them.
- [ ] Zoom into small text in the image: badges, receipts, signs. AI is weak there.
- [ ] Check the account's age and history: brand-new accounts with polished photos and instant intimacy are doing a job.
- [ ] For listings: same lighting/background across photos, reviews that mention the actual product, and buyers of a store that exists somewhere you can verify.
- [ ] One glitch found beats ten things that look fine. Trust the glitch.

## Before using a new AI tool or website

- [ ] Who told you the address? Ads and forwarded links are the weakest sources.
- [ ] Read the domain out loud, letter by letter, before signing.
- [ ] New tool → throwaway email and a password never used anywhere else.
- [ ] Permissions must match the job (a font generator needs neither your messages nor your contacts).

## If you already did the thing

- [ ] Stop contact; block the accounts; do not confront or negotiate.
- [ ] Money moved? **Call your bank now** — the number on your card. Report it as fraud.
- [ ] Passwords given? Change them everywhere they were reused; turn on two-factor login.
- [ ] Screenshot the evidence into one folder, then report: reportfraud.ftc.gov, ic3.gov, and the platform you were contacted on.
- [ ] Expect "recovery agents" to contact you. Anyone promising to recover your money is the same scam twice.
- [ ] Tell one person tonight. Shame is the scam's second product; naming it ends their leverage.
