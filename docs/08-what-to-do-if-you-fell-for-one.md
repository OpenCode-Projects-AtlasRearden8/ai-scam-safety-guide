# 08: What to Do If You Fell For One

Take a breath. This chapter was written on the assumption that good,
careful people sometimes do the thing — because it happened after
polite, machine-written pressure at an off hour, to someone tired. If
you're reading it in that moment, you're doing the most important step
already: acting now instead of hoping.

Shame is the scam's second product. Speed (acting) is the fix for the
first. Here is what to do, in order, in the first 24 hours.

## The first 15 minutes

1. **Stop all contact.** Do not message the scammer "back" to negotiate,
   warn, or confront. Do not reply at all. Block if you can.
2. **If money moved, call your bank or card now** — the number on the back
   of the card or your bank's app. Say: "I was scammed and made a payment;
   I want it treated as fraud." Ask specifically: can this transfer be
   reversed? Can the card be reissued? Note who you spoke to and when.
   Faster contact genuinely improves odds, especially under a day.
3. **If you gave a password, change it** — on the affected account, and
   anywhere you've reused that password (this is the honest cost of
   password reuse). Then turn on two-factor login: *The Privacy Comfort Handbook*'s chapter 03 has the calm how-to.
4. **If you gave a verification code**, treat that account's 2FA as
   neutralized for now: change its password and contact the platform's
   real support through their site or app, not any link from the scam.

## The rest of the first day

- **Freeze the possibility of more.** If you use the same password or a
  linked email elsewhere, change that. If a card was compromised,
  reissue rather than "keep an eye on it."
- **If you installed an app or opened a downloaded file:** uninstall the
  app; on a phone, also sign out of all sessions on your important
  accounts; if a full program ran on a computer from an unknown source,
  run your antivirus and consider resetting the device. When in doubt,
  a factory reset is safe for data you've backed up.
- **Gather evidence, calmly.** Screenshots of the conversation, the
  payment confirmations, the phone numbers, addresses and links, the
  times. A folder on your desktop named for the date. This helps the
  report (next) and any bank claim.
- **Report it.** US: [reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov) and
  [ic3.gov](https://ic3.gov) (FBI) take minutes. For mail-based scams,
  report to the postal inspection service. Report the platform too —
  marketplace, social app, dating site. And if money was significant, file
  a report with your local police; a police report number strengthens
  your bank dispute.
- **Tell one person.** Not the whole world if you don't want to. One
  trustworthy person is enough. They will say this could happen to anyone
  (it could — the scam was machine-made), and they can handle the calls
  while you're shaky.

## If intimate photos or chats were the leverage

Some scams (sextortion) threaten to share images unless you pay. Two
facts before any decision: **paying does not end it; paying funds the
next round.** Do not send money. Do not send more photos. Preserve
messages, block the accounts, report (the FTC center above handles this),
tell a person you trust — and if the victim is a minor, tell their
guardian and involve the police; the young person *is not in trouble here*.
This happens to thousands of people and they go on to fine lives.

## The second wave: recovery scams (the ugly sequel)

Within days or weeks, someone will contact you claiming they can recover
your money: "federal recovery agents," "blockchain consultants," "we
already caught the thief." **Anyone who contacts you promising recovery is
scamming a second time.** Real recoveries come only from institutions you
contacted, on channels you know. Nobody who cold-messages you about your
own bank dispute is on your side.

## The honest money-odds note

Recovered money depends on how fast you reported and which rail it went
over: card payments and platform purchases often get reversed or
compensated; gift cards, wire, and crypto rarely do. That's not a reason
to give up — report anyway (patterns help catch groups) — but set your
expectations kindly and put energy into what's next, not the loss alone.

## After the day is over

- Change your habits with the checklist — not as punishment, just as
  fitting new armor.
- Tell the family in Friday-talk terms (next chapter). Not "I was
  scammed" as a confession, but "here's what almost got me and how I now
  check," which is the strongest version of the lesson anyone learns.
- Let time be on your side. The feeling fades; the caution learned stays,
  and it will catch the *next* attack, which is now harder to run on you.

> You are not the story of the day you were scammed. You are the person
> who acted fast, reported it, armored up, and warned five people. That
> version is the one the rest of this guide exists to produce.
